## What the Prediction Cup actually rewards

The 2026 competition is unusually important to model correctly because **the objective function is not Sharpe ratio, drawdown, trade count, or even necessarily “good market making.”** Every participant begins with 100,000 SUSQies, trading ends at noon Eastern on November 4, 2026, and final rank among qualifying participants is determined entirely by the final SUSQie balance after settlement. First place receives $30,000, second $5,000, and third $2,500. citeturn8search3turn8search0

That changes what an optimal quant system should optimize.

Moloch should ultimately maximize something closer to:

\[
P(W_T > W_{\text{winning threshold}})
\]

rather than merely:

\[
E[\Delta W]
\]

where \(W_T\) is terminal SUSQie wealth.

That is especially important because your own October 14 internal benchmark recorded the then-first-place P&L at 952,904.16 SUSQies against a 100,000 starting bank. Your feasibility analysis calculated that simply repeating tiny one-tick maker gains at modest participation was nowhere near the required economic scale; under one deliberately favorable one-tick scenario at 1% participation it estimated about 163 SUSQies/day, while the reference gap required tens of thousands per day. fileciteturn5file0L2-L2

SIG explicitly permits automated strategies. The rules allow participants to access the API and connect an unlimited number of bots to their single account, subject to API rate limits, position limits, technical controls, the rules and terms. citeturn8search0 More bots do **not** multiply API capacity, however: since October 1, API rate budgets are account-wide, ordinarily 100 reads and 30 writes per minute; multiple API keys share that budget. citeturn8search1

SIG's market mechanics are richer than a simple:

> buy if bullish, sell if bearish

model.

A participant can submit market orders and resting limit orders. A participant can sell a side without already owning it, because selling YES is mechanically represented as buying NO at the complementary price. Buying the opposite side of an existing position cancels shares pairwise and immediately produces one SUSQie per cancelled YES/NO pair toward the transaction. A participant may also maintain resting orders on both sides simultaneously, although the venue prevents a user's orders from self-trading. citeturn7search2

This means Moloch should internally stop thinking of:

`BUY YES`, `SELL YES`, `BUY NO`, `SELL NO`

as four economically independent actions. They are transformations of **one binary payoff vector plus cash/collateral state**. citeturn7search2

That distinction matters enormously when searching for arbitrage.

There is also no realistic reason to pursue sub-millisecond execution on this venue. SIG says tournament-wide realtime exists through a single `markets_batch` channel, while REST books can be up to roughly one second stale and prices/trades/tournament details up to two seconds stale; SIG explicitly says polling faster than those refresh rates wastes rate budget. Realtime messages can also be dropped when they cannot be delivered promptly, requiring revision-gap detection and periodic REST resynchronization. citeturn5search9turn7search3

So I would classify the Cup as:

**high-frequency relative to human traders, but not exchange-colocation HFT.**

The right battleground is approximately one-second event-driven decision making with excellent inference and portfolio logic.

But the quant core is still fairly narrow. And Moloch's own evidence is warning you not to simply turn those strategies up.

Its first AR(1) experiment achieved prequential RMSE of 0.006007 versus 0.005810 for a zero-change prediction, so Moloch correctly rejected that as trading alpha. fileciteturn4file0L2-L2

Its flow research found one interesting asymmetric case—market 342/exchange 1031 had strongly negative conditional YES entry economics and positive NO economics in development—but when the NO hypothesis was frozen and tested prospectively, the first three qualifying future samples all produced zero 30-second liquidation gain. fileciteturn32file0L2-L2

The broader burst/markout research is even more revealing. In the later sample, 681 observations across 54 markets had an adverse rate of about 46.3% and a mean one-share delta of roughly **−0.01095**. Even after the history/concentration filters, the retained subset remained negative on mean markout. fileciteturn29file0L2-L2

The queue experiment demonstrates why fill count is misleading. Under identical event data, the control produced 47 hypothetical new fills and the conditional-queue variant 48, but both ended with about 746–747 SUSQies of owned inventory, an unresolved state, and **zero new realized profit**. fileciteturn30file0L2-L2

And when the inventory-horizon experiment forced that inventory flat after 30 or 60 seconds, the simulation became closed—but at **−100.520 SUSQies realized**, versus leaving the inventory unresolved. fileciteturn31file0L2-L2

This is the classic adverse-selection problem for passive market makers: **being filled is not necessarily success**. The trader willing to hit your quote can be doing so because your quote has just become wrong.

That pattern is entirely consistent with established limit-order-book research. Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov found short-horizon price changes more strongly linked to order-flow imbalance than raw trade volume, with the effect depending on book depth. citeturn12search0 Queue-reactive research similarly models execution probability and near-term price movement as conditional on the state of the queues rather than treating all resting orders as equally likely to fill. citeturn13academia6

So Moloch's next leap **is not “quote more aggressively.”**

It needs something that gives it an independent reason to know that an asset or package is mispriced.

Your structural study provides exactly that lead.

It found **117 matched Democratic/Republican race pairs**. In one frozen source prefix alone, there were **1,550 paired-book observations across 61 races where the executable dual-NO acquisition cost was below one**. The cheapest was 0.955 in MN-05. There was no immediate round-trip profit, which is why Moloch correctly described these as settlement-dependent structural economics rather than market-making profits. fileciteturn7file0L2-L2

For one share of a validated package:

\[
C = P_{\text{ask}}(NO_D) + P_{\text{ask}}(NO_R)
\]

If the rules prove that **exactly one** of the Democratic and Republican YES contracts will settle YES, then:

\[
\text{terminal payoff}(NO_D + NO_R)=1

\[

For the observed \(C=0.955\):

## The leap forward: a relationship-aware arbitrage engine

I would make **Graph Arbitrage** a first-class Moloch strategy above `pairedSpread`, `microprice`, and `cheapSpread`.

The key architectural change is that the unit of reasoning becomes a **payoff portfolio**, not an exchange.

### Build an authoritative contract graph

Moloch's all-market discovery should fetch each market's contract/node structure and then traverse SIG's `/relationships/graph` for eligible markets. Your merged code already recognizes both the market-nodes and relationships routes and scopes them to the SIG tournament. fileciteturn20file0L2-L2

Each tradable leaf becomes a payoff variable. The relationship graph becomes constraints such as:

\[
A \land B,\quad A \lor B,\quad \neg A,\quad A\Rightarrow B,
\]

or whatever exact relation types the SIG response actually documents.

Do not infer these constraints from market titles once an authoritative platform relation is available.

Title/NLP matching should be **candidate discovery only**. It must never be sufficient for a guaranteed-payoff classification. Your existing `STRUCTURAL_ECONOMICS.md` correctly identifies this weakness in the current screen. fileciteturn7file0L2-L2

### Compile contracts into terminal payoff vectors

For every connected graph component, enumerate the feasible terminal states \(\omega\).

For each purchasable YES/NO instrument \(j\), compute:

\[
A_{\omega,j}\in\{0,1,\text{refund-value}\}
\]

according to the actual settlement tree.

Then use executable book depth to assign marginal acquisition costs:

\[
c_{j,1},c_{j,2},\ldots
\]

This lets Moloch ask the much more important question:

\[
\min_x c^Tx
\]

subject to

\[
Ax \ge 1 \quad \forall \omega
\]

where \(x\) is the portfolio of shares.

Whenever:

\[
c^Tx < 1-\epsilon
\]

the bot has discovered a package whose minimum terminal payout exceeds its executable acquisition cost by more than safety margin \(\epsilon\).

For heterogeneous relationships, solve directly for maximum guaranteed profit:

\[
\max_{x,g} g
\]

subject to

\[
Ax-c^Tx\mathbf{1} \ge g\mathbf{1}.
\]

This is fundamentally different from forecasting a market's next tick. It searches for **internally inconsistent prices**.

### Start with deterministic templates before general optimization

The first production version does not need an elaborate general solver.

The most valuable templates are already obvious from your data:

**Party-pair arb.** Same election, Democratic-win and Republican-win contracts. Search both:

\[
Ask(NO_D)+Ask(NO_R)<1-\epsilon
\]

and, where settlement rules justify it,

\[
Ask(YES_D)+Ask(YES_R)<1-\epsilon.
\]

The second relation only works when the two contracts are proven collectively exhaustive; the first has its own requirements around third-party winners, cancellation and N/A conditions. Use the graph/rules, not intuition.

**Within-market complement transformations.** SIG directly guarantees YES/NO complement mechanics, including immediate cancellation of opposite holdings at one SUSQie per pair. That should be embedded into the optimizer rather than treated as a later accounting adjustment. citeturn7search2

**Cross-market implication packages.** Once the platform relationship graph proves a hard logical implication, Moloch can search for violations of inequalities such as:

\[
A\Rightarrow B\implies P(A)\le P(B).
\]

If executable prices violate the inequality sufficiently, a complementary portfolio can lock in the inconsistency.

**Larger connected components.** Only after the two- and three-leg system works should Moloch enumerate general graph portfolios.

### Make collateral part of the strategy, not merely accounting

This is potentially the biggest multiplier.

The generic Super Market product explicitly says its ALL system exists partly to solve capital inefficiency by recognizing guaranteed outcomes across related positions; its portfolio documentation describes a guaranteed payout floor together with an outstanding collateral advance. citeturn20search0turn20search2 And, crucially, your SIG-specific code already has access to both the relationship graph and portfolio-collateral reads. fileciteturn20file0L2-L2

The correct initial investigation is therefore a **read-only collateral census**, followed by an extremely small validated package test once the semantics are completely understood:

\[
CollateralBefore
\rightarrow
\text{Acquire validated package}
\rightarrow
CollateralAfter.
\]

Record gross cash, positions, relationship graph, transaction ledger and collateral state before and after.

You need to learn empirically:

\[
\Delta AvailableCash,
\quad
\Delta CollateralAdvance,
\quad
\Delta PayoutFloor,
\quad
\Delta GrossCash.
\]

Do not assume they are interchangeable. Your older execution code was deliberately conservative about this and explicitly demanded exact gross cash rather than treating collateral or marked balances as cash. fileciteturn16file0L2-L2

If SIG recognizes one of these portfolios and releases substantial collateral against its guaranteed payout floor, the strategic consequences are enormous.

Without collateral recycling, a 0.955→1.000 structural package earns 0.045 but occupies 0.955 until settlement.

With sufficiently favorable collateral recognition, much of that 0.955 could potentially become reusable buying power before final settlement. **I am intentionally not asserting the advance ratio, because the SIG public documentation I found does not specify it. That is what the endpoint experiment must establish.** The global platform documentation establishes the mechanism's existence; your SIG code establishes the endpoint's existence; neither source alone proves the exact Cup calculation. citeturn20search0 fileciteturn20file0L2-L2

That single result will determine whether this is merely a high-confidence 4–5% settlement trade or a potentially repeatable capital-velocity engine.

### Solve leg risk explicitly

Your existing structural document correctly warns that a pair displayed simultaneously is not an atomic fill. fileciteturn7file0L2-L2 Therefore Moloch's package executor should never do:

> buy leg A, hope leg B remains there.

Before leg one, calculate the worst permitted orphan loss from the full second-leg depth.

Let:

\[
E = 1-C
\]

be package edge and \(L\) the maximum modeled legging loss.

Require something like:

\[
E - \lambda L > E_{\min}
\]

where \(\lambda\) is deliberately conservative.

For the highest-confidence packages, a useful execution pattern is:

1. Rest passively on the less favorable/less liquid leg.
2. Keep continuously verified hedge depth on the complementary leg.
3. When the passive leg actually fills, immediately acquire the hedge using the freshest executable book.
4. Reject the trade beforehand unless worst-case hedge slippage still leaves positive package economics.

SIG supports both resting limits and immediate market orders, so this execution pattern uses documented participant functionality rather than unsupported order types. citeturn7search2

## Market making should become the execution layer

I would **not delete** Moloch's existing maker work. I would demote it.

Its purpose should become:

> acquire structurally desirable portfolios more cheaply and unwind unwanted inventory more efficiently.

This changes the market-making problem dramatically.

Today Moloch is effectively asking:

\[
\text{“Is this spread wide enough to quote?”}
\]

The new engine should ask:

\[
\text{“What is this share worth to my entire portfolio?”}
\]

A share of NO might be worth 0.40 to an ordinary trader but 0.44 to Moloch because acquiring it completes a portfolio with a guaranteed one-SUSQie floor. Conversely, another share may be worth *less* than its standalone fair value because Moloch already owns excessive correlated exposure.

That gives the maker an actual reservation value.

For contract \(j\), define:

\[
V_j^{portfolio}
=
V_j^{fair}
+
V_j^{structural}
+
V_j^{collateral}
-
V_j^{inventory}
-
V_j^{adverse}.
\]

The resulting quote can then be:

\[
Bid_j = V_j^{portfolio}-h_j
\]

\[
Ask_j = V_j^{portfolio}+h_j
\]

where \(h_j\) depends on observed queue state and fill risk.

This is where the existing microstructure work becomes useful.

Cont/Kukanov/Stoikov's order-flow-imbalance result suggests that recent additions, cancellations and executions at the best bid/ask carry more short-horizon information than raw trade volume alone. citeturn12search0 Queue-reactive models go further by explicitly conditioning arrival and execution probabilities on book state. citeturn13academia6

Moloch should maintain per-side features such as:

\[
OFI_t,
\quad
\frac{Q_{bid}-Q_{ask}}{Q_{bid}+Q_{ask}},
\quad
Q_{\text{ahead}},
\quad
\text{trade arrival intensity},
\quad
\text{cancel intensity},
\quad
\text{spread},
\quad
\text{last trade direction},
\quad
\text{time since revision},
\quad
\text{burst concentration}.
\]

For every candidate passive order it should estimate two separate quantities:

\[
P(\text{fill within }\tau\mid X_t)
\]

and

\[
E[\text{markout}_\tau\mid\text{fill},X_t].
\]

Then quote only if:

\[
P(fill)\times
(
spread\ capture
-
E[adverse\ markout\mid fill]
)
-
inventory\ cost > 0.
\]

That is much better than interpreting high fill probability as automatically desirable.

Moloch's own data strongly supports making this distinction. Its later burst dataset had substantial negative average markout, while its queue experiment produced many fills without realized profit. fileciteturn29file0L2-L2 fileciteturn30file0L2-L2

Avellaneda–Stoikov's classic market-making framework is useful here for its core principle: inventory should shift the maker's reservation value rather than merely trigger a fixed liquidation timer. citeturn12search7 But for Moloch I would replace the usual diffusion-based standalone price with the **graph-consistent portfolio value** above.

In other words:

**Avellaneda–Stoikov inventory control + queue-reactive fills + SIG relationship constraints.**

That combination is substantially more suited to this competition than a generic microprice bot.

## What could actually move Moloch toward first place

The present strategy family faces a scaling problem that your repository has already quantified. Tiny spread capture at conservative participation is orders of magnitude away from the reference leaderboard pace, while simply increasing participation exposes Moloch more aggressively to adverse selection. fileciteturn5file0L2-L2

The highest-upside path I found is therefore a combination of **structural edge and capital velocity**.

Determine whether SIG recognizes the package's guaranteed floor and releases usable collateral/buying power before settlement.

\textbf{Level C: maker acquisition}

Instead of paying both asks, use passive orders to improve one or both legs while continuously protecting the hedge.

\[
\textbf{Level D: flow intelligence}
\]

Use queue state and order-flow imbalance to decide when passive acquisition is safe and when to cross.

\[
\textbf{Level E: relative-value alpha}

When a package is not strictly guaranteed but its logical/cross-market fair value indicates a strong mispricing, trade it at a lower confidence tier.

\[
\textbf{Level F: directional election alpha}

Use independent election forecasting only for the remaining residual risk.
